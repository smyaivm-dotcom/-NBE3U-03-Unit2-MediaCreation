MAIN IDEA

What is the main idea of this piece?

The speaker's idea

At the start, legal documents are believed to give ownership. The older man challenges this by stating how the earth has always provided the necessity of life and that the earth will outlive the one being spoken to.

The Older Man's Challenge

The older man challenges this by stating how the earth has always provided the necessity of life and that the earth will outlive the one being spoken to.

The Main Idea

The main idea uncovers the difference between legally claiming ownership of land versus understanding the relationship between the Earth and the people who depend on it.

Evidence

Supporting Details & Quotes

"This land provides me with economic prosperity" (Laforme 14).

Land is seen as a source of money, describing it as "When I look upon this land it is money / as far as the eye can see" (Laforme 14).

→ This reveals that his perspective is solely based on profit and materialistic value.

"The old man looked at me with the saddest eyes / Bent and picked up a handful of dirt / and said, you actually believe those lies" (Laforme 14).

→ The old man challenges the idea that a piece of paper can give connection to land.

"This gave life to you and me / And provided for everything you have and / everything you see" (Laforme 15).

→ Land is essential to life, it is beyond currency.

"This is the reason for our birth" (Laforme 15).

Hence the name, “Mother Earth.”

Earth is not something for humans to control or profit in. We are beneficiaries, not permanent owners.

MESSAGE/THEME

Earth is not for humans to control/profit in. It existed before us, provides the necessities we need to live, and will continue to even after our lives have ended. We are beneficiaries of the earth, not permanent owners, and we need to recognize our responsibility to care for something that sustains us and will sustain those to come.

ANSWER: Legal ownership can be given through a land registration system. The land system of a given province, for example, Ontario, contains all the information about ownership of the specific piece of property and other rights that may be associated with it. Aboriginal title, however, differs from that as it is a collective right that secures an Indigenous group’s ownership of land. Aboriginal title includes the right to occupy, possess, and use the property and receive any economic benefit from it. The exact rights of aboriginal title may depend on legal and historical factors, and there are Indigenous peoples’ land rights that can be found as treaties.

ANSWER: The answer depends on the land and the rights that apply to that land. Where aboriginal title is proven, the Indigenous that owns the title has the right to decide how that land is used and managed including for modern uses. The title is subject to the rule that the land cannot be developed in a way that takes away benefits from generations. Although the governments also have authority, they have the duty to talk with Indigenous groups, and where needed, make adjustments when government decisions might harm claimed, proven Aboriginal, or treaty rights. This means that decisions about land cannot always be seen as something one person or government has control over, rather, they all work together to ensure a safe place for all.

Critical Literacy

Critical Literacy & Devices

Critical Literacy

Critical Literacy Analysis

The intended audience

I think this piece is aimed towards those who think land is a means of ownership and transactions or those who need/are looking for enlightenment. This is because of the final line, “But we both know who owns who,” (Laforme 15) begins a question in the reader's mind, forcing them to reflect on their own values and possibly adopt new, accurate ones.

The overall tone

  • Sounds very confrontational, interrogative, and “saddest eyes” reflect disappointment.
  • The irony in “But we both know who owns who.” (Laforme 15) makes someone reflect and renew their perspective.

The purpose of this poem

  • I believe the purpose of this poem is to challenge the belief that owning land legally means having control over it.
  • Presents two perspectives from two people (this could possibly represent colonizer vs an Indigenous person), giving readers the opportunity to see both points of views and decide which is most humane according to their values.

How language (diction) is used in “Mother Earth”

  • The author makes the old man use philosophic language to fix the speaker's perspective.
  • “money as far as the eye can see” (Laforme 14) shows that the speaker measures the value of land through its potential profit.
  • “one day reclaim you” (Laforme 15) suggests that the earth will eventually take humans back into its natural cycle.

Does Chief R. Stacey Laforme have any identifiable bias or sway of thought and how does that change the way that we understand the topic?

The old man’s perspective takes the majority of the poem. This changes the way we understand the topic because the author only goes into depth on one topic, highlighting all the old man’s thoughts. Chief R. Stacey Laforme is known to be a distinguished poet and storyteller from the MCFN, going back to what I mentioned earlier, I think that the old man represents the Indigenous communities, which is why I think Laformes gives emphasis on his part in order to share his own perspectives through a character.

How the poem is written/structured and why it matters

  • Dialogue/conversation between two people
  • Rhymes
  • Sounds like a story

This matters because a dialogue allows for two perspectives, which helps us consider both sides. The rhythm and story-like format makes it seem like it is directed towards a broader audience, whether those seeking more knowledge on Indigenous communities or those who like to read. Unlike a “venting” poem which does not necessarily target readers' understanding.

Literary Devices

Persuasive & Literary Devices

1. Irony

Device

“But we both know who owns who.” (Laforme 15)

This switches the initial perspective at the beginning of the poem, making readers think into their own values. Although you may have believed that you own/control the land, we all know by the time this statement is given that humans are dependent on the earth, therefore, it is not something we have the authority to control.

2. Personification

Device

“The Earth nurtures you and will one day reclaim you” (Laforme 15)

Earth is described as something that can nurture and reclaim people. To readers this creates the sense that earth is its own separate being with its own will. It is not something we can control.

3. Metaphor

Device

“When I look upon this land it is money” (Laforme 15)

The speaker describes the land as if it is money itself, showing that there is its value through profit. Though the land itself is not literally money; this metaphor shows how the speaker is reducing the earth to an economic benefit.

My Journal Reflection

Slowing down when reading allows me to understand a text beyond the apparent. In “Mother Earth” by Chief R. Stacey Laforme, the conflict is ostensibly about who owns who, but the ending surfaces a deeper inquiry about whether humans can own something they depend on and that will continue beyond their lives. Probing the author's choice of word and persuasive device choice helps uncover meanings that might have been missed during a cursory reading. Additionally, different texts we have studied that address similar topics have demonstrated how perspective and experience influence the way people understand the world. In “Walking Both Sides of an Invisible Border,” Alootook Ipellie writes about the experience of living between two cultures, bringing light to how a person's relationship with culture can affect their identity and sense of belonging. In “Mother Earth” by Chief R. Stacey Laforme, different perceptions of land, whether as profit or recognizing it as a source of life, challenged readers to reconsider the concept of ownership. Our learning on the Doctrine of Discovery substantiated how European beliefs and laws influenced treatment of Indigenous relationships to the land, irrespective of Indigenous peoples having their own ways of understanding that relationship. While all these texts relate to land, they delve into different experiences and ideas from within that relation. Together, they have taught me that understanding an issue requires consideration of history and human experiences that shape it, as well as looking beyond one perspective.

To get more out of texts, I can reread important passages, ask questions about the author's intent, analyze persuasive devices they utilize, and connect ideas across different works. When unacquainted historical and cultural questions arise, I can also apply the CRAAP test to evaluate whether information is relevant and trustworthy so I provide reliable sources to back my research. Before taking this course, I conceptualized identity as attributes that make a person identifiable or unique, such as their ethnicity, race, name, or personality. I now appreciate that one's identity can also be defined by their language, history, culture, experiences, and their relationship with the land and community. This has helped me recognize that identity is not only how a person is described but can also reflect the experiences and events that have shaped their life. Because of this, the first three weeks of the course have gone well as I am more comfortable identifying themes, analyzing persuasive device choices, and relating text to one another. While working on “Mother Earth” by Chief R. Stacey Laforme, I was able to develop questions about land, ownership, and responsibility from the poem's core message. However, I wish to improve my ability to formulate meaningful research questions while still having answers that can be supported by credible and factual evidence as some questions tend to invite assumptions due to the lack of evidence. Moving forward, my goal is to ask questions that spark deeper thinking but do not rely on assumptions, so that my interpretations are thoughtful and my factual claim is accurate.
